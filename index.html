Konrad's Journey

5 Days in Istanbul.
Learning to let go of the Grid.

"I work, I plan, I like structure. I travel from time to time, but mostly in ways that feel familiar. I rely on schedules and reservations. This week in Turkey, however, I learned that the best things happen when you lose the plan."

Day 1 • 17:00 • The Metro Line M2

Talking to Strangers

I had just landed and was dragging my suitcase onto the M2 metro line. My mind was busy calculating the route to my hotel, checking stops, ensuring I wasn't being pickpocketed. I was in my "transit bubble."

Then, the bubble popped. A man sitting next to me—mustache, leather jacket, kind eyes—tapped my shoulder. "Tourist?" he asked. I nodded politely, expecting him to sell me something. Instead, he smiled. "Welcome to Istanbul. My name is Mehmet."

In Germany, we have an invisible wall around us in public transport. Breaking it requires a valid reason—a delay, an accident, or a fire. Here, the reason was simply existence. By the time I reached my stop, Mehmet had given me his phone number and made me promise to visit his home for dinner later that week. I took the number out of politeness, never intending to use it. Or so I thought.

🇩🇪 Germany

  • Friendship requires time & specific context (clubs, work).
  • Privacy in public spaces ("Coconut Culture").

🇹🇷 Turkey

  • High spontaneous social interaction.
  • Strangers are seen as potential friends ("Peach Culture").

* * *

I spent the next two days doing what Germans do best: sightseeing by the book. Hagia Sophia. Blue Mosque. Galata Tower. It was beautiful, but distant. Then, on Day 4, my phone buzzed. It was Mehmet. "Dinner tonight. My wife is cooking. No excuses."
My rational brain said: Don't go. You don't know him.
My gut said: Go.

* * *

Day 4 • 18:30 • Üsküdar Neighborhood

More Than a Handshake

I found the apartment in a winding street in Üsküdar. When Mehmet opened the door, my German autopilot engaged. I stood straight, made eye contact, and extended my right hand for a firm, professional handshake—the universal sign of respect in my world.

Mehmet ignored my hand completely. He stepped into my personal space, grabbed my shoulders, and pulled me into a hug, kissing both my cheeks. "Hoş geldin, brother!" he boomed. Inside, his cousins and nephews were waiting. The greeting ritual repeated with everyone. Touches on the shoulder, warm smiles, lingering handshakes.

I felt stiff, awkward, like a robot that hadn't been programmed for affection. In Germany, we keep distance to show respect. Here, they destroy distance to show welcome.

🇩🇪 Germany

  • Low Contact Culture.
  • Handshake maintains professional distance.

🇹🇷 Turkey

  • High Contact Culture.
  • Physical touch (hugs, kisses) signals trust.
Day 4 • 19:30 • The Feast

A Guest Is Never “Just” a Guest

I expected a simple meal. What I got was a culinary assault. The table was groaning under plates of Dolma, Börek, salads, and meats. I barely knew these people, yet they treated me like a long-lost king.

Every time my plate was half-empty, Mehmet's wife refilled it. When I politely said, "No, thank you, I'm full," they laughed and put more food on my plate. I realized that in Turkey, "No" is just the start of a negotiation. To accept the food is to accept the love.

In Germany, hospitality is a contract: You invite me, I bring wine, we eat what is planned, I leave at a reasonable hour. Here, hospitality is a total surrender. I was the "Tanrı Misafiri"—the Guest of God. My comfort was their moral duty.

🇩🇪 Germany

  • Reciprocity is expected.
  • Visits are planned with clear time limits.

🇹🇷 Turkey

  • "Tanrı Misafiri" (Guest of God).
  • Total service without expectation of immediate return.
Day 4 • 20:30 • The Living Room

Family First

The apartment was loud. Not aggressive loud, but happy loud. Cousins dropped by unannounced. Neighbors walked in to borrow sugar and stayed for tea. The concept of a "Nuclear Family"—just parents and kids behind a locked door—didn't seem to exist here.

They asked me about my family. "Where do your parents live?" Mehmet asked.
"In a retirement home near Munich," I said.
The room went silent. They looked at me with genuine confusion, bordering on pity. "Why not with you?"
I tried to explain our concept of independence, of not being a burden. They didn't buy it. To them, family is the safety net, the bank, and the identity. To be independent is to be alone.

🇩🇪 Germany

  • Individualism.
  • Focus on the Nuclear Family.

🇹🇷 Turkey

  • Collectivism.
  • Extended Family is the primary identity.
Day 4 • 21:00 • The Uncle Arrives

When Age Enters the Room

The door opened again, and an elderly man with a cane and a prayer bead (tesbih) walked in. It was Mehmet's uncle. The change in the room was instant and electric.

Every single young person stood up immediately. The loud laughter stopped. One by one, the cousins approached him, bowed slightly, kissed his right hand, and pressed it to their forehead. Even Mehmet, the loud joker, became humble and quiet.

In my office in Munich, respect is earned by competence, KPIs, and titles. Here, the uncle didn't need to do anything. His age was his rank. He was the history of the family, and that demanded reverence.

🇩🇪 Germany

  • Merit-based authority.
  • Egalitarian approach to age.

🇹🇷 Turkey

  • Seniority-based authority.
  • Ritualized respect (Hand-kissing, standing up).
Day 4 • 22:30 • The Balcony

When the Day Actually Begins

We stepped onto the balcony for a smoke. I looked at my watch: 10:30 PM. In Germany, the sidewalks would be rolled up. Curtains closed. The sacred "Ruhezeit" (quiet time) would be in full effect.

Here? The street was exploding with life. I saw grandmothers drinking tea on benches. Teenagers on scooters. A group of men playing Backgammon, the dice clattering loudly. Toddlers were running around playing tag.

"Don't these people sleep?" I asked.
Mehmet laughed. "Sleep is for when you are dead. Now we live."
While Germany sleeps to be productive tomorrow, Istanbul stays awake to be connected today.

🇩🇪 Germany

  • Strict work-rest separation.
  • "Ruhezeit" (Quiet hours) are legally protected.

🇹🇷 Turkey

  • Polychronic time.
  • Social life peaks after sunset.
Day 5 • 09:05 AM • The Silence

Silence at 9:05

I stayed at Mehmet's place that night. The next morning, we walked towards the ferry. Suddenly, at exactly 9:05 AM, a siren wailed across the Bosphorus.

Then, the impossible happened. A city of 16 million people froze. Cars slammed on their brakes in the middle of the bridge. Drivers got out and stood at attention. Pedestrians stopped mid-step. The only sound was the wind and the siren.

It was November 10th—the death anniversary of Atatürk.
I got goosebumps. Coming from Germany, where patriotism is a quiet, difficult, and often critical topic, this overwhelming, collective wave of emotion was shocking. It wasn't forced; it was felt.

🇩🇪 Germany

  • Constitutional Patriotism.
  • Critical/Reserved relationship with symbols.

🇹🇷 Turkey

  • Emotional Patriotism.
  • Collective rituals reinforce national unity.
Day 5 • 14:00 • The Tea Garden

Talking Without Saying Everything

Before I left for the airport, Mehmet told me about a problem he had with his boss at the textile shop. "I told him that perhaps, if God wills, the new shipment might be difficult to sell if the color isn't quite right," Mehmet said.

"Why didn't you just say: 'Boss, you ordered the wrong ugly color'?" I asked.

Mehmet looked horrified. "And shame him? No. He understood what I meant."
I realized that my German directness—my obsession with "Facts"—would be a bull in a china shop here. In Turkey, truth is wrapped in layers of cotton to protect the relationship. You read the air, not just the words.

🇩🇪 Germany (Low Context)

  • Direct, factual.
  • Separation of person and problem.

🇹🇷 Turkey (High Context)

  • Indirect, metaphorical.
  • Preserving "Face" and harmony is priority.
Day 5 • 16:00 • To the Airport

A Moving Conversation

The taxi ride back was the final exam. We were weaving through traffic at high speed. Cars were millimeters apart. To my German eyes, it was anarchy. There were lanes, but no one used them. There were signs, but no one obeyed them.

But then I looked closer. It wasn't chaos. It was a high-speed negotiation. Drivers were constantly making eye contact, using small hand gestures, honking not to say "I hate you" but "I am here."
In Germany, I trust the traffic light (The System). Here, I had to trust the other driver (The Human). And surprisingly, it worked.

🇩🇪 Germany

  • Rule-Based.
  • The System is the authority.

🇹🇷 Turkey

  • Relationship-Based.
  • The Driver/Negotiation is the authority.

* * *

Back in Germany, the silence of my apartment felt different. It wasn't peaceful anymore; it was just empty. So I picked up my guitar to make sense of the noise, the tea, and the chaos.

* * *

Epilogue • The Reflection

The Grid and the Flow

Words weren't enough to capture the feeling of the Bosphorus wind or the sound of the dice on the backgammon board. This is my summary – not in a spreadsheet, but in a song.
